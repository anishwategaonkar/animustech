What happened

A search we ran closed in fifteen days. Offer released, offer accepted, candidate on a sixty day notice period. On the last day of that notice, he received another offer paying more than ours. He backed out. The position reopened.

Fifteen days of search work, sixty days of waiting, and nothing to show for it except a client starting again from the top.

The fix the client asked for, and why it does not work

The client's response was immediate and completely reasonable on the face of it: from now on, only show us candidates with thirty day notice periods. Shorter window, less time to be poached.

We do not think that works. A candidate who intends to shop an offer will shop it in seven days as readily as in sixty. The behaviour is not created by the length of the window, it is created by having a written offer in hand that proves what someone is willing to pay. Halving the window halves the time available, not the intent.

What the thirty day filter definitely does is cut a large share of the available market. Notice periods of sixty and ninety days are ordinary in India, particularly in larger organisations and particularly at senior levels. Screening them out removes a meaningful part of the candidate pool in exchange for a risk reduction that we do not believe is real.

That is a trade worth making consciously, if at all. Most clients make it in the week after a back-out, when the memory is fresh and the reasoning is emotional rather than tested.

What an accepted offer actually is

Here is the reframe that we think does the most work.

An accepted offer letter is not a commitment. It is a price quote, in writing, that the candidate can now take to the market as proof of what a serious employer will pay them. Your number becomes your competitor's benchmark. All a competing firm has to do is beat it, and they know exactly what beating it requires.

Which means the notice period is not a countdown to joining. It is a window of exposure, and the length of that window determines how much exposure, not whether there is any. Clients almost universally read it the other way round: offer signed, box ticked, seat filled, move on to the next thing. The recruitment industry encourages that reading, because for most agencies the invoice is triggered by the offer, not by the joining.

Where notice sits in your timeline maths

This matters for how you judge whether a search was actually slow.

On the sales associate search, twenty five days from intake to someone sitting at the desk. Ten of those days were the search. Fifteen were the candidate's notice. On the SaaS marketing manager search, roughly twenty seven days intake to joining, of which twelve were the search and fifteen were notice.

Any agency quoting you twenty five days without breaking out the notice period is managing your impression rather than your search. The part worth holding a recruiter to is the part they control, and notice is not it. What they do inside the notice period, on the other hand, is entirely within their control, and that is where most of them do nothing at all.

What we changed

Two things, neither of them clever.

A fixed contact cadence through notice. A call once a week, and the call goes from us, not left to the client to remember. We also schedule a proper meeting during the notice period so that it is a real conversation about how things are going rather than a check-in ping that gets answered with one word. The point is not surveillance. It is that a candidate who has been in genuine conversation with someone for eight weeks is in a different position, when a competing offer lands, than one who signed a letter and then heard nothing.

The runner-up stays warm until day one. We do not treat a search as closed on offer acceptance. We treat it as closed on joining. That means keeping the second-choice candidate in a real conversation rather than sending a polite rejection the moment the first one signs.

Both of these cost us time after the point at which most agencies consider the work finished. That is precisely why most agencies skip them. If your fee lands on offer release, the notice period is somebody else's problem.

We should be straight about the limits of this. We introduced both practices after the back-out described above, and we are not going to claim a success rate we cannot yet evidence. What we can say is that the two changes follow directly from the diagnosis: if notice is a window of exposure rather than a countdown, the only sane responses are to stay present inside the window and to keep an alternative alive until the risk has actually passed.

What you can do on your side

  • Stop treating acceptance as closure. The seat is filled when someone sits in it. Everything before that is a forecast.
  • Keep the hiring manager in contact. A ten minute call from the person the candidate will actually report to, once during notice, does more than anything a recruiter can send. It is also the cheapest thing on this list.
  • Do not shorten the notice window as your fix. It narrows your market without narrowing your risk. If you want to filter on something, filter on whether the candidate can articulate why they are leaving, which at least tells you whether money is the only lever holding them.
  • Ask your agency what happens between offer and joining. If the answer is vague, the honest translation is that nothing happens, and you will find that out the expensive way.
  • Keep the runner-up warm yourself if nobody else will. Restarting a search costs more than a slightly awkward holding conversation.

The uncomfortable part

None of this makes a back-out impossible. A candidate who receives a materially higher offer on day fifty nine may well take it, and no amount of weekly calls changes the arithmetic if the gap is large enough. What the practices above change is the share of cases where the decision was close, and the candidate had no relationship with anyone at the hiring company to put on the other side of the scale.

The thing worth internalising is simpler than any of the tactics. The offer is not the end of the search. It is the point at which your own number starts working for your competitors, and the weeks that follow are the ones where the hire is actually won or lost.
